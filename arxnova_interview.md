## If we gave you a raw portfolio tape from an originator tomorrow, how would you go from messy data to an investment view?

1) validate the data
2) understand what the portfolio actually is
3) analyse performance drivers
4) make assumptions
5) translate assumptions into cashflows/losses
6) stress the downside
7) compare risk versus return / structure
8) identify what you still need to know

- I’d probably think about it in stages.

- First, I’d want to make the data usable and trustworthy. So that means cleaning the tape, checking definitions field
  by field, identifying missing values, inconsistencies, duplicates, and making sure I understand exactly what each
  variable represents. At that stage I’d also want to know what information is genuinely available versus what is being
  inferred, because that affects how much confidence you can place in the analysis.

- Second, I’d try to build a basic factual picture of the portfolio. What kind of assets are we looking at, what are the
  key borrower and loan characteristics, how is the pool distributed by vintage, geography, product type, seasoning, LTV
  if relevant, coupon, term, arrears status, and so on. The objective there is to understand what the portfolio actually
  consists of and where the concentrations are.

- Third, I’d move to performance analysis. I’d want to look at delinquency trends, defaults, recoveries, prepayments,
  roll rates, vintage behaviour, and any signs of underwriting drift or adverse selection. That helps form a baseline
  view of how the collateral has behaved historically and what the main risk drivers seem to be.

- Then I’d translate that into assumptions for a cashflow or loss model — base case first, then downside scenarios.
  Depending on the asset class, that could mean assumptions around default timing, severity, recoveries, prepayments,
  and servicing performance. The point is to turn raw portfolio data into expected cashflows and downside loss outcomes
  rather than just descriptive statistics.

- After that, I’d stress it. I’d want to see how sensitive the investment case is to weaker recoveries, higher defaults,
  slower collections, macro deterioration, or other relevant shocks. That’s where you start to understand not just the
  expected return, but how robust the position is if things go wrong.

- Finally, I’d connect the analytics to the investment question: what does this imply for value, required yield,
  downside protection, and where the risks really sit? And just as importantly, I’d be clear about what uncertainties
  remain — for example data limitations, missing history, or areas where we’d need further diligence from the originator
  or servicer.

- So for me, the process is really about moving from messy information to a structured view on collateral quality,
  downside risk, and whether the return adequately compensates for that risk.

## Suppose you joined and in the first month we gave you a pool from a lender with incomplete data, limited history, and a tight timeline. How would you handle the uncertainty without slowing the team down?

1) triage,
2) what is good enough for a first decision,
3) clear flags,
4) parallel workstreams,
5) no false precision.

- I’d handle it in a staged way, with a strong focus on being decision-useful rather than waiting for perfect
  information.

- First, I’d quickly triage the data quality: what is reliable, what is missing, and which gaps are cosmetic versus
  genuinely decision-critical. The key is not trying to solve everything at once, but identifying what we can trust and
  where the main uncertainties sit.

- Second, I’d build a high-level view of the pool as quickly as possible — asset type, key concentrations, borrower or
  loan characteristics, vintage mix, arrears status, and any obvious risk pockets. Even with incomplete history, that
  usually gives you an initial sense of what kind of exposure you are looking at.

- Third, I’d run a simple but disciplined base case and a set of downside scenarios using conservative assumptions where
  the data are weak. In that kind of situation, I think it’s better to be roughly right and explicit about uncertainty
  than to create false precision.

- And throughout, I’d make the uncertainties very clear to the team: what we know, what we don’t know, what assumptions
  are driving the result, and what further diligence from the originator or servicer would matter most. That way, the
  team can move quickly, but with a realistic understanding of the downside rather than false confidence.”
